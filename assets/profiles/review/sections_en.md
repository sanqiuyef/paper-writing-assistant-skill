# Review-Paper Section Templates

## 1. Abstract

State the review question, scope, databases or materials, search and screening method, taxonomy, main consensus/disagreement, and research agenda. A systematic review should report the search cutoff and included-study count; never invent counts before screening is complete.

## 2. Introduction

Define the topic, terminology, and importance; compare the time, object, or method boundaries of prior reviews; and state the review questions. Identify the work as narrative, scoping, or systematic and do not label an undocumented search process as systematic.

## 3. Related Work

Summarize conceptual development and prior reviews, then justify the analytical taxonomy. Avoid duplicating the later synthesis; explain why the selected dimensions answer the review questions.

## 4. Methodology

A systematic review should report databases, dates, full search strings, language/year limits, eligibility criteria, deduplication, title/abstract screening, full-text screening, extraction, and quality or bias assessment. A narrative review should still state source-selection boundaries.

## 5. Experimental Design

Treat this as evidence-synthesis design: define the coding sheet, classification dimensions, outcomes or comparison indicators, quantitative/qualitative synthesis, and consistency checks. For meta-analysis, add effect size, heterogeneity, sensitivity, and publication-bias methods.

## 6. Results and Analysis

Report screening and study distribution before thematic or methodological synthesis. Compare evidence direction, strength, data conditions, and scope; do not substitute publication counts for evidence quality. Separate consensus, conflict, and missing evidence.

## 7. Discussion

Interpret consensus and disagreement across categories, identify methodological limitations, data gaps, and field bias, and assess limitations of the review itself. Make the research agenda specific in population/object, data, comparison, outcome, or validation.

## 8. Conclusion

Summarize reliable consensus, critical evidence gaps, and priority directions in a limited set of points. Avoid claims of comprehensive coverage beyond the search scope or strong conclusions from consistently weak studies.
